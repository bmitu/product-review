Lodge all Lodge cookware — Nothing published that a reader could check

Lodge says: “We know that our cookware has been nontoxic since the very beginning, but we also lean on vetted, third-party labs to verify that our cookware is free not only of PFAS, but also dangerous levels of Lead and Cadmium.”

their own page · read 2026-09-26

UNSUPPORTED — we went and checked; here is what we found · checkable by reading a document

Bottom line. Lodge says third-party labs verify its cookware is free of dangerous levels of lead and cadmium. It names no lab and shows no result. Its own disclosure lists cadmium compounds in red and orange enameled pieces.

Evidence

  • our own desk check — mixed, we are not a neutral party here

    https://www.lodgecastiron.com/blogs/story/non-toxic-cookware-cast-iron-made-without-pfas

    Lodge's blog post read in full, plus a sweep of its site for any published lab report; the post names no lab and links no result.

    The claim tells readers that labs have checked, and gives them nothing to check. There is no lab name, no report and no figure for what 'dangerous' means. That is not evidence the testing did not happen. It means a reader has only Lodge's word that it did.

  • document — mixed, funded by Lodge Cast Iron

    https://www.lodgecastiron.com/pages/california-disclosure

    Lodge's California disclosure page read in full: its enameled cast iron lists cadmium compounds for red and orange pieces.

    Lodge's own disclosure is what gives the word 'dangerous' weight. For its enameled cast iron, red and orange pieces only, the chart lists cadmium compounds, which are used to make those colours. California's disclosure law covers handles and food-contact surfaces, so the cadmium is somewhere on the pan a person touches or cooks on, and the page does not say where. Lead does not appear anywhere in the chart. So the sentence is not a promise that Lodge cookware contains no cadmium. For some of it, Lodge's own paperwork says it does. The promise is that the amount is not dangerous, and that is exactly what an unpublished test cannot show a reader.

  • document — mixed, funded by Lodge

    https://web.archive.org/web/20191019110833/https://www.lodgemfg.com/use-and-care/frequently-asked-questions

    Lodge's old FAQ, archived 2019, named the FDA leach test for lead and cadmium in glazed surfaces, with no results. It is gone from the current site.

    This is the test that would answer the question: it measures how much lead and cadmium an acid solution draws out of the enamel, which is what matters for a red or orange pot. So Lodge has, at least in the past, said which test it relies on. It never published a result, a lab or a date, and the statement is no longer on its site.

  • measurement — mixed, independent

    https://tamararubin.com/2019/01/light-blue-white-lodge-cast-iron-enameled-dutch-oven-48-ppm-lead-35-ppm-arsenic

    Posted in 2019: a 2015 handheld X-ray fluorescence reading of one Lodge enameled Dutch oven lid, read on the tester's own site.

    This is one of two independent measurements we found, from the same tester, and it needs reading with care. It is one lid, of unknown age, tested in 2015. An XRF reading measures what is in the surface, not how much of it can leach into food, which is what food-safety limits for enamel are built on. It does not measure cadmium on a red or orange piece, which is the question the disclosure raises. And the tester is an advocate who believes enameled cast iron should always be avoided, which she says plainly. What it does show is that lead has been found in the food-contact enamel of a Lodge piece, even though lead does not appear in Lodge's current materials chart. That chart lists what Lodge adds on purpose, so the two are not necessarily in conflict. They do show why a published leach test would be worth more than a sentence saying one exists.

  • measurement — mixed, independent

    https://tamararubin.com/2019/01/dark-blue-white-lodge-cast-iron-enameled-dutch-oven-1693-ppm-lead-555-ppm-arsenic

    Posted in 2019: a 2016 handheld X-ray fluorescence reading of one Lodge enameled Dutch oven bought around 2006: 1,693 ppm lead in the interior.

    This is a much higher lead reading than the other piece, and it is on the cooking surface. The same limits apply: one pan, about twenty years old, measured for what is in the enamel rather than what leaches out, by a tester who opposes enameled cast iron in general. It does not show that a Dutch oven Lodge sells today has lead in it. It does show that lead has been found in Lodge's interior enamel more than once, which is exactly what a published leach result would speak to.

The sentence is carefully worded, and the wording matters. It does not say Lodge cookware contains no cadmium. It says labs have verified there are no 'dangerous levels' of it. Lodge's own California disclosure explains the care: its red and orange enameled cast iron is listed as containing cadmium compounds, the pigments that give those colours.

Cadmium in a coloured enamel is not unusual, and a well-fired enamel can hold it tightly. Whether it does is a question about how much leaches out into food, and that is what the lab testing Lodge describes would answer. Until 2019 Lodge's FAQ named the right kind of test: the FDA method that measures how much lead and cadmium leaches from a glazed surface. It never published a result, named the lab, or said what threshold it treats as dangerous, and the FAQ is gone from its current site. The disclosure says where to look. The test that would settle it is one Lodge says it runs and has not shown.

Two independent readings add to the question without settling it. A consumer advocate's handheld tests in 2015 and 2016 found lead in the white interior enamel of two Lodge enameled pieces: 48 parts per million in a lid of unknown age, and 1,693 in a Dutch oven bought around 2006. They measured what is in the enamel, not what leaches out, on two older pieces.

For a shopper the practical reading is narrow. Lodge's bare seasoned cast iron lists no cadmium or lead at all, and independent testing of it has come back clean. The open question is about the enameled pieces, and it would take a published leach test to close it.

  • document — lodgecastiron.com

    Read on 2026-09-26 in the HTML the server sends. The page says 'Our cookware is made without PFOA and PTFE' and then gives a chart of every material in each product type, including ones California does not list. Seasoned cast iron (Classic, Bakeware, Blacklock, Chef Collection, Lodge BOLD, Grilling & Outdoors): iron, phosphorus, manganese and manganese compounds, strontium, carbon, silicon, sulfur, steel, and soybean-oil seasoning. Seasoned carbon steel: steel and soybean-oil seasoning. Enameled cast iron adds antimony compounds, steel, nickel and nickel compounds, copper and chromium compounds, and, for 'Red and Orange items only', cadmium compounds and sulfate. Glass lids: manganese.

  • document — web.archive.org

    Read on 2026-09-26 from the Internet Archive's capture of 19 October 2019 (the page was captured from 2016 to 2019 at this address, and in 2013 at an older one). Under 'Are Lodge Enamel products tested for lead safety?' it said: 'Lodge utilizes the U.S. Food and Drug Administration Test Procedure 7.5.1.4a Leachability of Lead and Cadmium for Glazed Ceramic Surfaces. The FDA Division of Compliance Programs uses ASTM-C738 as the Standard Method of Test for glazed ceramic. In addition to information provided by vendors, Lodge Manufacturing Company uses third-party testing to ensure that products with the Lodge name comply with standards set forth by the United States Food & Drug Administration.' It also claimed compliance with California's Proposition 65. A sweep of 105 pages on Lodge's current site found no mention of the method; the only cadmium on the current site is the disclosure chart.

  • measurement — tamararubin.com

    Read on 2026-09-26. The post, dated 14 January 2019, says: 'I tested this light blue Lodge enameled cast iron dutch oven lid back in 2015', and that 'I did not record the estimated year of manufacture for this piece.' Readings from an XRF instrument: blue exterior enamel, lead non-detect and arsenic 35 ppm; white interior food-surface enamel, lead 48 ppm and arsenic non-detect. The same author reports Lodge's plain, unenameled cast iron as consistently free of lead, cadmium, arsenic and mercury in her testing since 2015. Corrected 2026-09-27: this item previously described the reading as a 2019 test of a Dutch oven; the post says it was a lid, tested in 2015.
