T-fal PTFE nonstick cookware — Does not hold up

T-fal says: “PFOA is a short-chain PFAS.”

their own page · read 2026-09-26

SUPPORTED — evidence exists; nobody independent has measured the product · checkable by reading a document

Bottom line. T-fal's PFAS explainer calls PFOA a short-chain PFAS. It is the standard example of a long-chain one, and falls squarely inside the EPA's long-chain definition. The error is in the explanation, not in T-fal's claims about its pans.

Evidence

  • document — refutes, independent

    https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-40/chapter-I/subchapter-R/part-721/subpart-E/section-721.10536

    The EPA rule defining long-chain perfluoroalkyl carboxylates, read in full from the official electronic Code of Federal Regulations.

    The EPA's rule for long-chain perfluoroalkyl carboxylates covers acids of the form CF3(CF2)n-COOH with n from 6 to 20. PFOA, perfluorooctanoic acid, is CF3(CF2)6-COOH: n is 6, so it is the shortest member of the long-chain class, not a short-chain one. This is not a fine point of chemistry. PFOA is the textbook example of a long-chain PFAS.

  • our own desk check — refutes, we are not a neutral party here

    https://www.t-fal.com/ptfe-nonstick-coating-explained

    T-fal's PTFE explainer page read in full; the sentence sits under the heading 'What is PFOA?'.

    The error does not change what T-fal says about its own pans, which is that PFOA has not been used since 2012. It matters for a different reason. The page is teaching readers how to sort PFAS into harmful and harmless, and it gets the most famous example wrong in the definition.

A small error, on a page whose purpose is to explain. T-fal's page on PTFE sets out to help readers tell the PFAS 'known to be harmful', such as PFOA, apart from fluoropolymers like the PTFE in its pans. In doing so it calls PFOA a short-chain PFAS. By the EPA's regulatory definition, PFOA is long-chain: an eight-carbon acid, the shortest one inside the long-chain class.

None of this touches T-fal's statement that it stopped using PFOA in 2012, which we have not checked here. It is worth recording because a reader who takes the page as a primer comes away with the categories confused, and because a brand explaining the chemistry of its own coating should be expected to get the chemistry right.
