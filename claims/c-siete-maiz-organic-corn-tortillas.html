Siete Gluten Free Maíz Organic Yellow Corn Tortillas (9.7 oz) — Nothing published that a reader could check

Siete says: “Rooted in heritage, these Organic Maíz Yellow Corn Tortillas carry the timeless taste of gathering. Each bite celebrates the delicious flavor of organic corn, elevated with a sprinkle of savory sea salt and a touch of organic olive oil”

their own page · read 2026-09-23

UNSUPPORTED — we went and checked; here is what we found · checkable by reading a document

Bottom line. Siete's tortillas carry the USDA Organic seal and name their certifier, as the rules require. But the maker holding the certificate is not named, and the USDA's public register has no entry under Siete, so it cannot be traced.

Evidence

  • document — mixed, independent

    https://organic.ams.usda.gov/Integrity/MonthlyReports/INTEGRITY_Export_20260901.xlsx

    The USDA's full list of organic operations for September 2026, all 78,701 rows, searched with a control name that it did find.

    The USDA's register of organic operations is the document that should settle this, and it does not contain the name on the package. No certified operation is listed as Siete, under its own name or as another name, and no certified operation mentions Siete in its list of products.

    That is less damning than it sounds, and the reason is in the organic labelling rules themselves. They require a package to name the certifier of 'the handler of the finished product' — the company that made it — which need not be the brand on the front. A brand can have its food made by another company. The register lists the makers, not the brands they make for, so a brand that does not make its own tortillas would not appear here under its own name even if everything were in order.

    What the register does show is how many candidates there are. The certifier named on this package lists 84 certified operations with at least one product whose name contains the word 'tortilla', from tortillas themselves to chips and soup. Some of those operations list the brands they produce for by name. None of them names Siete.

  • our own desk check — mixed, we are not a neutral party here

    https://cdn.shopify.com/s/files/1/0074/1939/6181/files/Maiz_Tortilla_BackYCTO-1500.png

    The back of the package as the brand shows it on its own product page: it names the certifier and the distributor, but not the maker.

    The package does what the federal labelling rule requires of it. It carries the organic seal, marks each organic ingredient, and names a certifier — Quality Assurance International, which appears as the certifier on 2,735 of the register's entries — in the place the rule specifies, under the distributor's name.

    What it does not do is name the company that holds the certificate. The rule does not require that, and this package does not. But it is the missing link between the label and the public register: the certifier certifies many operations, and without the maker's name a reader has no way to pick out which of them made these tortillas, or to check that its certificate is current and covers this product.

This is the most ordinary claim we have checked, and the result is worth having precisely because it is ordinary. 'Organic' on a US food label is not marketing language. It is a legally defined term, backed by a federal register of every certified operation, and a label that uses it must name who certified the product.

This package does all of that. We still could not trace the certification from the label to the register.

The reason is structural, not a failing of this brand in particular. The rule asks the label to name the certifier of whoever made the finished product, and allows the brand on the front to be only the distributor. The register lists makers. So the chain a reader needs — this package, made by this company, certified by this body, current as of this date — has a missing link in the middle, and the label is not required to supply it.

We want to be clear about what this does not mean. It is not evidence that these tortillas are not organic. A false organic claim on a national brand, carrying the USDA seal and naming a real certifier, would be a serious violation, and nothing here suggests one. What it means is narrower: the public register exists so that anyone can check an organic claim, and for this product, from the outside, nobody can. The claim rests on the seal and on trust in the system behind it.

The fix is small and entirely in the brand's hands. Printing the maker's name, or the certificate number, would close the chain.

How we checked

  • document — organic.ams.usda.gov

    Downloaded on 2026-09-23 from the USDA's organic database, which publishes its complete list of certified and formerly certified operations as a monthly spreadsheet. The database's search page only works in a browser, so we used the full file instead: 78,701 operations across 68 columns. We searched every column except street addresses for the word 'Siete' in any capitalisation and found no match. As a control, the same search for an operation we had seen listed on the database's own front page found all five of its entries. The word 'Siete' does occur in the file, but only inside addresses, as the Spanish word for seven.

  • our own desk check — cdn.shopify.com

    Read on 2026-09-23 from the package images on the brand's product page. The front carries the USDA Organic seal. The back reads 'Distributed by Siete Foods, Austin, TX 78704' and, beneath it, 'Certified Organic by QAI'. The ingredient list marks the corn and the olive oil as organic and leaves water, sea salt and lime unmarked; the rules forbid calling water or salt organic. No manufacturer or facility is named anywhere on either panel.
