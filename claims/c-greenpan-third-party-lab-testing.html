GreenPan Thermolon ceramic nonstick coating — Nothing published that a reader could check

GreenPan says: “Our Thermolon™ coating and GreenPan™ cookware undergo independent testing by highly professional, accredited third-party labs to ensure compliance with our product claims, quality standards, and numerous safety regulations.”

their own page · read 2026-09-20

UNSUPPORTED — we went and checked; here is what we found · checkable by reading a document

Bottom line. GreenPan says accredited third-party labs test its coating. Across all 212 pages of its site there is no report, no certificate number and no laboratory named — and unlike some rivals, it never promised to publish them.

Evidence

  • our own desk check — mixed, we are not a neutral party here

    https://www.greenpan.us/sitemap_pages_1.xml

    A sweep of all 212 pages in the brand's own sitemap, every one fetched successfully, finding no report, certificate or laboratory name.

    The claim says testing happened. It does not say the results are published, so the check is not whether a promise was kept — it is whether a reader who wants to see the evidence can. Sweep the brand's own sitemap and see:

    curl -sL 'https://www.greenpan.us/sitemap_pages_1.xml?from=79290859688&to=122787627176' \
  | grep -oE '<loc>[^<]+</loc>' | sed 's/<[^>]*>//g' \
  | while read u; do curl -sL "$u" | grep -oiE 'href="[^"]+\.pdf[^"]*"'; done | sort -u

    That returns eight documents across 212 pages: cookbooks for the skillet, slow cooker, grill and ice cream maker, two care-and-use booklets, and a lifetime warranty. No test report. Searching the same 212 pages for the names of the large testing houses — Intertek, SGS, Eurofins, Bureau Veritas, TÜV, Smithers, NSF — returns nothing at all.

    So the position a reader is left in is this. Four specific standards are named on the site: US FDA 21 CFR 175.300, European Framework Regulation 1935/2004/EC, the German LFGB Sections 30 and 31, and elsewhere a list of four certifications covering the FDA, German LFGB, the Swiss government and KTR. Those are real, checkable standards. What is missing is the one thing that would connect the product to them — a report, a certificate number, or the name of the body that issued it. A standard named without the document that certifies against it is a citation to a library with no call number.

    One further detail, offered because it cuts the other way and belongs in the record. On a separate page the company says: "We have a dedicated in-house testing lab that compares our innovations with other products on the market." That is a straightforward and unusually candid statement — many brands describe in-house work in language that implies independence. It is also, by definition, not third-party testing, and the two kinds of testing sit on different pages without anything telling a reader which one produced which claim.

This is the same test we ran on Caraway, pointed at a brand that had just passed a different one — and the result deserves to be read carefully, because the two cases are not the same and it would be easy to file them together.

Caraway said, in its own words, that it publishes its reports. It names a page. We went to that page and the reports were not in what the server sends. That is a promise measured against its own terms.

GreenPan makes no such promise. It says testing is done by accredited third-party laboratories, which is a claim about what happens in a factory and a laboratory, not a claim about what appears on a website. Our sweep cannot refute it, and nothing here should be read as suggesting the testing did not happen. What the sweep establishes is narrower and still worth a reader's attention: there is no route from the claim to the evidence. A reader who wants to see for themselves has nowhere to go.

Why that matters more here than it would elsewhere. This is the brand whose California chemical disclosure we checked and found genuinely published — ten itemised tables, one link from the footer of every page. So the company clearly can publish compliance documentation, has the material to publish, and has chosen to publish it in one domain where the law requires it and not in the other where it does not. That is not hypocrisy; it is the ordinary shape of disclosure everywhere, and it is exactly why a publication like this one exists. Voluntary transparency stops at the edge of the mandate with striking regularity.

The honest summary: unverifiable from outside, through no broken promise. The next move is not another sweep — it is to ask the company for the reports and publish whatever comes back, including nothing.

How we checked

  • our own desk check — greenpan.us

    We swept the brand's own site on 2026-09-20 — all 212 pages listed in its sitemap, every one fetched successfully — looking for a test report, a certificate, or the name of a laboratory. We found none of the three. Eight pages carry a PDF; all eight are recipe books, care-and-use booklets or a warranty. What we did NOT do is ask the company for the reports, which is a different check and a fair one that we have not yet run.
