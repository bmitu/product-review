Blue Lizard Sensitive Mineral Sunscreen Lotion SPF 50 (3 oz and 5 oz) — Holds up — but only on the seller's own evidence

Blue Lizard (Crown Laboratories) says: “We ♥ the Reef: This product is made without Oxybenzone or Octinoxate to help protect fragile reef ecosystems.”

their own page · read 2026-09-23

SUPPORTED — evidence exists; nobody independent has measured the product · checkable by reading a document

Bottom line. Blue Lizard says this sunscreen is made without oxybenzone or octinoxate, and the full ingredient list it filed with the government names neither. That is the maker's own filing, not a lab test.

Evidence

  • document — supports, funded by Crown Laboratories (the maker files its own label; the National Library of Medicine publishes it)

    https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=32d78048-d281-1d80-e063-6394a90aec19

    The full ingredient list the maker filed for this sunscreen names neither chemical; the same search finds oxybenzone on a label that contains it.

    Sunscreen in the US is regulated as a drug, so its Drug Facts label is a legal filing, not marketing copy, and the maker has to list every ingredient. That makes the filed label the right document for a 'made without' claim. Two commands reproduce the finding:

    curl -sL 'https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/services/v2/spls/32d78048-d281-1d80-e063-6394a90aec19.xml' | grep -ciE 'oxybenzone|benzophenone|octinoxate|methoxycinnamate'
0

curl -sL 'https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/services/v2/spls/598cfe9e-6ebe-ad18-e063-6294a90ace22.xml' | grep -ciE 'oxybenzone|benzophenone|octinoxate|methoxycinnamate'
4

    The first is this sunscreen, and the answer is zero. The second is an unrelated sunscreen that does contain oxybenzone, and the same search finds it, which shows the search works.

    What this is and is not matters. The label is the maker's own statement, filed with the government, and a false one would make the product misbranded under federal law. That is a strong reason for it to be accurate, but it is still the maker describing its own product. Nobody measured what is in the tube, including us. The label shows what the maker says it uses, not a lab result.

  • our own desk check — mixed, we are not a neutral party here

    https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=9d20b230-2cce-4d90-e053-2995a90a4c03

    The product page's barcodes belong to an older formula filed under a second label; that formula also names neither chemical.

    The product page describes one formula and scans as another. Its ingredient list, with aloe, matches the newer label. Its barcodes match the older one. Both labels are live on the government database, and the store does not say which formula a given order will contain.

    For this claim it does not matter: neither formula lists oxybenzone or octinoxate, so the claim holds whichever tube arrives. It would matter to anyone buying for a different reason, such as avoiding a particular preservative, because the ingredient list on the product page may not be the one on the tube. The label on the tube you receive is the one to read.

The claim has two halves, and we checked one. The first half is a fact about the formula: the product is made without two particular sunscreen chemicals. That can be checked, because US sunscreens are sold as drugs and the maker must publicly file a complete ingredient list. The filed list for this product names neither chemical, under any of their names, in either of the two formulas currently on file. The brand's wider statement that all Blue Lizard sunscreens are free of both also held: none of the 21 Blue Lizard labels on file names either one.

The second half, 'to help protect fragile reef ecosystems', is a statement about why, and about what leaving the two chemicals out does for coral. Hawaii banned both chemicals from sale for that reason, but whether a sunscreen without them is actually better for reefs is a question for marine science, not for a label. This record does not decide it.

One practical note: the product page shows the newer formula's ingredients but carries the older formula's barcodes, and both formulas are still on file. If you are choosing this sunscreen for reasons beyond these two chemicals, read the label on the tube you receive.

How we checked

  • document — dailymed.nlm.nih.gov

    Read on 2026-09-23 from DailyMed, the National Library of Medicine's public database of drug labels, through its public API. Sunscreens are sold in the US as drugs, and the maker must file the label, with every active and inactive ingredient, in a structured format. This label is 'Blue Lizard Sensitive SPF 50 Sunscreen', effective 31 December 2025. Its active ingredients are zinc oxide (112 mg/mL) and titanium dioxide (89.6 mg/mL); it lists 28 inactive ingredients, including aloe leaf, matching the 'new and improved formula with soothing Aloe Vera' and the ingredient list on the brand's product page. We searched the whole file for oxybenzone and octinoxate under their other names (benzophenone-3, octyl or ethylhexyl methoxycinnamate): no match. As a control, the same search run on another maker's label that lists oxybenzone found it 4 times. We did not test the product.

  • our own desk check — dailymed.nlm.nih.gov

    Read on 2026-09-23. The brand's store lists two sizes, 3 oz and 5 oz, with barcodes 303162046306 and 303162046450. A sunscreen's barcode carries its drug product code, and these decode to 0316-2046-30 and 0316-2046-45. Those two package codes are not on the label cited above, which covers 0316-2046-31 (89 mL) and -46 (148 mL). They are on a second, live label for the same product name, effective 12 January 2026, covering -30 (89 mL) and -45 (148 mL). That label has the same active ingredients at the same strengths but a different set of 28 inactive ingredients: no aloe, and it includes phenoxyethanol and chlorphenesin. Searched the same way, it names neither oxybenzone nor octinoxate.
