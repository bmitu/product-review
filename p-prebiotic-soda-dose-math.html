Prebiotic soda: the number on the can, and the number the claim needs

Two numbers decide whether a prebiotic soda does what its label implies, and only one of them is printed on the can. Here is the arithmetic, the published threshold it runs against, and the single cheap lab test that would close the gap. No lab was needed to get this far — only the label and one regulator's published condition of use.

OLIPOP OLIPOP (company-level certification) — Nothing published that a reader could check

OLIPOP says: “On July 12, 2023, OLIPOP became a Certified B Corp with a score of 91, marking our steadfast dedication to positive global impact.”

their own page · read 2026-09-22

UNSUPPORTED — we went and checked; here is what we found · checkable by reading a document

Bottom line. OLIPOP says it was certified as a B Corp on 12 July 2023 with a score of 91. The certifier's public directory would settle it, and the company even links its own entry, but that directory will not serve the page to anyone checking.

Evidence

  • our own desk check — mixed, we are not a neutral party here

    https://www.bcorporation.net/en-us/find-a-b-corp/company/olipop/

    We could not read the certifying body's own directory: every request, plain or browser-dressed, met a challenge page at the front door.

    Not verified against the primary source. We tried to look this up in the certifying body's own public directory on 2026-09-22 and could not read it. The address is not one we had to guess: the company links its own directory entry from the footer of its website, so the exact page that would settle the claim is identified. Every request we made to it — plain, and dressed as an ordinary web browser with the full set of headers a browser sends — came back as a challenge page rather than the listing, from the directory's own front door. We tried the parent domain without the subdomain and a plausible programmatic address for the same data, and both were refused the same way. So nothing in this record confirms or contradicts the certification, the date or the score. What we can report is that we went to the one place that would answer it, and that the public cannot read it without a browser session.

    This claim is put better than most we check. It names the certifying body. It gives an exact date. It gives a number, 91, which is the kind of figure that is either right or wrong. And the company links its own entry in the certifier's directory from its website, so a reader is not left guessing where to look. Nearly everything that usually makes a certification claim uncheckable has been removed here by the company itself.

    And we still could not check it.

    The certifier's public directory — the register that exists precisely so that anyone can confirm who is certified — would not serve us the page. Not the company's listing, not the directory's front page. What comes back is a challenge screen asking the requester to prove it is a person using a browser, which a reader with a browser will pass without noticing and which stops any attempt to check systematically, or to check and show the receipt.

    We want to be careful about what this does and does not say. It is not evidence that the certification is wrong. It is not a criticism of the company, which has done the part that is in its control. It is a statement about a register: a directory whose purpose is public verification, that a member of the public cannot retrieve.

    The practical effect is that this claim, as things stand, rests on the company's own word — not because the company failed to offer proof, but because the proof it points at is behind a door we cannot open and show you through.

Most certification claims we look at fail for a boring reason: the brand names a standard and never says which certificate is theirs, so there is nothing to look up. This one is the opposite, and that is what makes it worth publishing.

OLIPOP gives the certifying body, the date, and the score. It links its own entry in the certifier's directory from its own website. Every piece of information a reader would need to check the claim has been handed over. On the face of it this is a model of how to make a claim checkable.

The register is the part that fails. The directory exists to let anyone confirm who holds the certification — that is its entire function — and it would not serve us the listing. What arrives instead is a screen that wants the visitor to demonstrate they are a person at a keyboard. Someone clicking through casually will pass it and may never know it was there. Anyone checking carefully, or wanting to show a reader the receipt rather than ask them to take it on trust, stops here.

We have now run the same method against four certification and disclosure claims across three brands, and it fails in two distinct ways that are worth separating, because they need different fixes.

In the first, the brand names a scheme but not its own certificate. There is a public register, and no way to find the brand in it. Nothing the register's owner does can fix that; only the brand can, by printing its certificate number.

In the second — this one — the brand identifies its entry precisely and the register will not be read. Nothing the brand does can fix that; only the register's owner can.

Both end in the same place for a reader, which is why they are easy to confuse: a claim that looks backed by an outside authority and cannot actually be traced to one. But only claims where both halves work can be checked for nothing, by anyone, in an afternoon. Two of the four we tried cleared both halves, and those two produced firm findings — one against a brand, one in a brand's favour. The other two produced this: a record of a door that was locked.

We are publishing that rather than quietly dropping it. A claim we could not check is a different thing from a claim that failed, and a reader is entitled to know which of the two they are looking at — including when the reason is that a public register was not, in practice, public.

OLIPOP Vintage Cola, 12 fl oz (355 mL) — Nobody has measured this

OLIPOP says: “higher fiber per can to get more out of every sip”

their own page · read 2026-09-20

UNSUPPORTED — no qualifying evidence · checkable only by a new independent measurement

Bottom line. The can advertises more fiber, but how much of that fiber is the prebiotic kind is a trade secret. One lab assay per batch — about $360 — would turn it into a number anyone can check.

What would settle it: Quantify total fructans (inulin + FOS) in sealed cans by AOAC 997.08 (HPLC-RI), three production lots, reported against the declared 9 g of total dietary fiber — turning the undisclosed prebiotic fraction of OLISMART into a published number.
Estimated cost: $1,080

Evidence

  • None exists. Nobody has run the measurement described above — that is the finding.

This is the cheapest decisive test we have found. One assay, one product, one number, and the claim on the next record stops being un-dividable and becomes arithmetic a reader can redo. ‘More fiber per can’ is what the assay bites on: a dose-benefit claim whose dose is published only in aggregate.

No brand will pay for it, and that is structural rather than cynical. The only thing the result can do is narrow a claim the brand currently gets to leave open. That is the signature of a test that only readers will ever fund.

THE PRICE, and where it comes from. $1,080 = 3 production lots × $360.00, which is Medallion Labs' published per-analysis price for Fructans/Inulin by AOAC 997.08 HPLC-RI (7–9 business days, 15 g sample). The figure appears identically in Medallion's 2026 price catalog and on its per-test page, so it is the lab's own standing price for exactly this analysis. It is not a quote against our samples — no request was sent — and the catalog notes that prices are subject to change without notice.

Two bracketing sources, for honesty about how firm that number is. LA Testing / EMSL lists fructan (FOS + inulin) by HPLC at $460–920 per sample and by spectrophotometric method at $300–600 — but that schedule is Rev 8.1 dated 2011-07-29, fifteen years stale, so treat it as a bracket and not a price. Measurlabs does not offer fructan or inulin analysis at all; its dietary fiber determination is AOAC 991.43 inside a €380 nutritional package. Two of the three sources converge on Medallion's figure being the current one, and the third cannot do the test.

A DESIGN OPTION we have priced but not included above. The 9 g on the label is TOTAL dietary fiber, and the method behind that number is not disclosed. Medallion states that its Rapid Integrated Method (AOAC 2022.01, $580/analysis) ‘can accurately analyze samples that contain RS2/RS3 fibers, Oligosaccharides, Inulin/Fructans, Polydextrose, and a variety of other fiber ingredients’ — which implies the choice of fiber method matters when fructans are present. Re-measuring the denominator alongside the numerator, 3 lots × ($360 + $580), would cost $2,820 and would check the label's own 9 g rather than taking it on trust. It roughly triples the target, so it is a separate decision.

OLIPOP Vintage Cola, 12 fl oz (355 mL) — The published numbers do not reach it

OLIPOP says: “Our proprietary prebiotic fiber & botanical blend to support your digestive health.”

their own page · read 2026-09-20

UNSUPPORTED — we went and checked; here is what we found · checkable by arithmetic over published numbers

Bottom line. One can declares 9 g of total fiber against the 12 g/day EFSA set for the inulin claim, so a can cannot reach that dose even if every gram were inulin — and the real inulin figure is undisclosed.

  • Claimed: 9 g per 355 mL (12 fl oz) can, counting TOTAL dietary fiber as the product's own Nutrition Facts declare it
  • Studied: 12 g/day of native chicory inulin — the condition of use EFSA attached to the authorised claim 'Chicory inulin contributes to maintenance of normal defecation by increasing stool frequency' (EFSA Journal 2015;13(1):3951)

Evidence

  • arithmetic — refutes, funded by OLIPOP

    https://drinkolipop.com/products/vintage-cola

    The upper bound is decisive without any new measurement. The can declares 9 g of TOTAL dietary fiber, so the inulin in it is at most 9 g, against the 12 g/day EFSA attached to the only authorised inulin claim of this kind. One can therefore cannot deliver that dose even in the impossible case where every gram of fiber is chicory inulin. The real figure is lower by an undisclosed margin: OLISMART is a proprietary blend listing cassava root fiber, chicory root inulin, Jerusalem artichoke inulin, nopal cactus, marshmallow root, calendula flower and kudzu root with no per-ingredient amounts, and cassava root fiber is listed first, ahead of both inulins.

    Not verified against the primary source. efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com returned HTTP 403 to automated fetch on 2026-09-20, so the figures here come from the published abstract and the authorised-claim wording rather than the full text. We are saying so because a publication whose premise is that receipts are retrievable does not get to quietly cite one it could not itself retrieve.

    Independent of OLIPOP, and the threshold cuts against its own applicant's commercial interest: an inulin manufacturer applied for the claim, and the Panel set the bar at 12 g/day across six studies in 86 subjects.

The interesting part is that OLIPOP does not commit the ordinary version of this failure. The label arithmetic is honest: 9 g of fiber is 9 g of fiber, declared with its denominator. The gap sits one layer up. The benefit claim is about PREBIOTIC fiber; the number on the can is TOTAL fiber; and the prebiotic fraction is a trade secret. So the claim is not false — it is un-dividable. A reader cannot do the arithmetic because the brand declines to publish the numerator.

Two honest qualifications. First, the EFSA figure is for chicory inulin specifically, and OLISMART contains chicory root inulin plus Jerusalem artichoke inulin, so the comparison is in-kind rather than exact. Second, the claim as worded promises ‘support’, names no threshold and makes no per-can promise; that is why the verdict is ‘unsubstantiated’ and not ‘refuted’. What is refuted is the reading a shopper would take from a 9-g-fiber front label.

One cheap assay would make the full arithmetic possible — the same product appears in our unmeasured-claims backlog for exactly that reason.
