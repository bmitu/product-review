Ceramic nonstick

What a ceramic nonstick cooking surface is and what is known about it, one sourced sentence at a time; then every product line we list with this surface, and the claims we have checked about them. We do not say which to buy.

How we decide a line's surface: The cooking surfaces of the frying pans and skillets the brand sells, in the brand's own words. We say PTFE or ceramic only where the brand's own page does (Teflon, a trademark for PTFE coatings, counts as naming PTFE); otherwise the coating is 'not named'.

What it is and what is known

  1. Researchers describe the coatings sold as 'ceramic' nonstick as silica particles held in a siloxane, a silicone-type binder.
    Source: RSC Advances (Royal Society of Chemistry), 2026, via PubMed Central, “Non-stick performance of polymethylsilsesquioxane thin films synthesized by sol–gel spray coating” (peer-reviewed study), read 2026-09-30.
    Their words
    “These coatings consist of silica particles embedded in a siloxane matrix” (Introduction; the sentence directly follows 'They are often marketed as non-stick “ceramic coatings”.')
  2. One commercial sol-gel nonstick coating for cookware and bakeware is mixed from silane chemicals, water, an acid catalyst, polymer particles and fillers, then cured by heating above 200 °C, according to a review written by PPG, a company that makes and sells sol-gel coatings.
    Source: Journal of Sol-Gel Science and Technology (Springer), 2022, via PubMed Central, “Industrial applications of sol–gel derived coatings” (peer-reviewed study, not independent), read 2026-09-30.
    Their words
    “The sol–gel coating composition is based on one or more organoalkoxysilanes such as methyltrimethoxysilane, an acid catalyst for hydrolysis reaction, water, organic polymer particles such as polyphenylene sulfide (PPS), and fillers. The coated panel is then cured above 200 °C, such as at 327 °C for 30 min.” (Section 'Non-stick coatings for cookware and bakeware')
  3. In a 2024 lab test that baked pancake batter 90 times on each of four commercial coatings, lifting the pancake off the two ceramic siloxane coatings took 15 to 34 kPa, against under 3 kPa for PTFE and PFA, the fluoropolymer coatings.
    Source: Journal of Food Engineering (Elsevier), 2024 (abstract via Europe PMC), “Evaluation of the effectiveness and durability of commercial non-stick coatings” (peer-reviewed study), read 2026-09-30.
    Their words
    “The results have shown that perfluoroalkoxyalkane (PFA) and polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) were the most effective in terms of pull-off pressure (<3 kPa) versus ceramic siloxane coatings with values between 15 and 34 kPa after 90 firings.” (Abstract)
  4. A 2017 study of commercial nonstick frying-pan coatings made of titanium dioxide and quartz particles in a silicone binder found that titanium-containing particles moved into water and mildly acidic test liquids held in contact with the coating for 2 hours at 100 °C, at up to 147 micrograms per square decimetre of surface.
    Source: Food Additives & Contaminants: Part A, 2017 (abstract via the U.S. National Library of Medicine's PubMed), “Characterisation of food contact non-stick coatings containing TiO2 nanoparticles and study of their possible release into food” (peer-reviewed study), read 2026-09-30.
    Their words
    “Characterisation revealed that the coatings contained micron- and nanosized rutile TiO2 particles, and quartz SiO2 embedded in a silicone polymer matrix. In order to estimate possible migration of TiO2 nanoparticles from coatings into food, migration tests into simulants (deionised water, 3% acetic acid and 5 g l-1 citric acid) were performed (2 h at 100°C), and thermal and mechanical degradation of the matrix was studied. Simulants were analysed by ICP-MS after ultrafiltration and by microwave-assisted digestion. The concentration of titanium-containing particles that migrated into simulants was up to 861 µg l-1 (147 µg dm-2).” (Abstract)
  5. In a study published by the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology, a commercially sold nanoceramic-coated frying pan released more titanium and silicon into an acidic test liquid after simulated washing, scouring and scratching than when new. Titanium dioxide and silica nanoparticles were detected only after the harshest scratching.
    Source: U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology (paper in Food Control, 2018), “Consumer Use Effects on Nanoparticle Release from Commercially Available Nanoceramic Cookware” (government research), read 2026-09-30.
    Their words
    “Commercially available nanocoated fry pan and nanoclay sauce pot were evaluated for nanoparticle migration under three consumer use conditions. Washing, scouring, and scratching conditions were simulated by linear abrasion using scrubbing pads, steel wool and tungsten carbide burr attachments, respectively. Migration was evaluated using 3% acetic acid as food simulant. Titanium (Ti) and Silicon (Si) concentrations in simulant were generally higher under the consumer use scenarios than in fresh/unused pans. Titanium dioxide (TiO2) and silicon dioxide (SiO2) nanoparticles were detected under the most aggressive use scenario simulated by abrasion with the tungsten carbide burr attachment.” (Abstract)
