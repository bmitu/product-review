What it is and what is known

  1. Stainless steel is a family of steels. What they all share, according to worldstainless, the steel industry's stainless association, is chromium: at least 10.5% by weight.
    Source: worldstainless (the World Steel Association's stainless steel programme), “Introduction to stainless steels” (industry group, not independent), read 2026-09-30.
    Their words
    “The one key element they all share is a certain minimum percentage (by mass) of chromium: 10.5%.”
  2. The '18/10' stamped on many stainless pans describes the steel's make-up: 18% chromium and 10% nickel.
    Source: European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), “Update of the risk assessment of nickel in food and drinking water (EFSA Journal, 2020)” (regulator), read 2026-09-30.
    Their words
    “18/10 steel contains 18% chromium and 10% nickel.” (Footnote 21)
  3. Stainless grades differ in how much nickel they hold: grade 304 has about 18–20% chromium and 8–12% nickel, grade 316 about 16–18% chromium and 10–14% nickel.
    Source: Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry (Kamerud, Hobbie and Anderson), via PubMed Central, “Stainless steel leaches nickel and chromium into foods during cooking (Kamerud, Hobbie and Anderson, Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry, 2013)” (peer-reviewed study), read 2026-09-30.
    Their words
    “SS grade 304 contains approximately 18–20% mass fraction chromium, and 8–12% nickel, whereas SS grade 316 contain approximately 16–18% chromium, and 10–14% nickel” (Introduction)
  4. In a 2013 laboratory study, tomato sauce cooked for six hours in contact with stainless steel ended up with up to 26 times more nickel and 7 times more chromium than sauce cooked without it; how much depended on the grade of steel.
    Source: Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry (Kamerud, Hobbie and Anderson), via PubMed Central, “Stainless steel leaches nickel and chromium into foods during cooking (Kamerud, Hobbie and Anderson, Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry, 2013)” (peer-reviewed study), read 2026-09-30.
    Their words
    “After six hours of cooking, Ni and Cr concentrations in tomato sauce increased up to 26- and 7-fold respectively, depending on the grade of stainless steel.” (Abstract)
  5. In the same study, new steel released the most. The release fell with each repeated cooking and levelled off after the sixth, but it did not stop.
    Source: Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry (Kamerud, Hobbie and Anderson), via PubMed Central, “Stainless steel leaches nickel and chromium into foods during cooking (Kamerud, Hobbie and Anderson, Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry, 2013)” (peer-reviewed study), read 2026-09-30.
    Their words
    “Cooking with new stainless steel resulted in the largest increases. Metal leaching decreases with sequential cooking cycles and stabilized after the sixth cooking cycle, though significant metal contributions to foods were still observed.” (Abstract)
  6. Europe's food safety authority, reviewing the evidence on nickel in food, cites a study in which tomato sauce cooked for one hour in stainless steel pots that had been in home use for 10 to 12 years took up nickel from them: 66 to 144 micrograms per litre across three brands.
    Source: European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), “Update of the risk assessment of nickel in food and drinking water (EFSA Journal, 2020)” (regulator), read 2026-09-30.
    Their words
    “Tomato sauce and lemon marmalade were cooked for 1 h in used pots from three different stainless steel brands. The pots had been used in a domestic setting for 10–12 years. Cooking tomato sauce for 1 h resulted in releases of 66, 144 and 98 μg/L” (Section 1.3 (occurrence), summarising Guarneri et al. 2016)
