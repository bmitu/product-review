What it is and what is known

  1. PTFE (polytetrafluoroethylene), also sold under the brand name Teflon, is used in cookware for its non-stick properties and its stability at high heat. Overheated, it gives off toxic fumes that can occasionally cause fluid in the lungs.
    Source: Journal of Medical Case Reports (Hamaya et al., 2015), “Polytetrafluoroethylene fume-induced pulmonary edema: a case report and review of the literature” (peer-reviewed study), read 2026-09-30.
    Their words
    “Polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE), or Teflon® (DuPont, Wilmington, DE, USA), is ubiquitous in materials commonly used in cooking and industrial applications owing to its thermal stability and non-stick properties. However, overheated PTFE generates toxic fumes that can occasionally cause acute pulmonary edema” (Introduction)
  2. PTFE is a fluoropolymer, and scientists class fluoropolymers as one of the groups of polymers within the PFAS family.
    Source: Environmental Science & Technology (Lohmann et al., 2020), “Are Fluoropolymers Really of Low Concern for Human and Environmental Health and Separate from Other PFAS?” (peer-reviewed study), read 2026-09-30.
    Their words
    “Within the groups of polymeric PFAS, there are fluoropolymers, side-chain fluorinated polymers, and poly- or perfluoropolyethers. As defined by Buck et al. (2011), “fluoropolymers” represent a distinct subset of fluorinated polymers, based on a carbon-only polymer backbone with F atoms directly attached to it, e.g., polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE)” (Section 1, Introduction)
  3. Historically, the chemicals most widely used as processing aids in making fluoropolymers such as PTFE were salts of PFOA and PFNA.
    Source: Environmental Science & Technology (Lohmann et al., 2020), “Are Fluoropolymers Really of Low Concern for Human and Environmental Health and Separate from Other PFAS?” (peer-reviewed study), read 2026-09-30.
    Their words
    “Historically, the most widely used polymer processing aids were the ammonium salts of perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA) and perfluorononanoic acid (PFNA).” (Section 2)
  4. The US EPA says the companies in its voluntary 2010/2015 PFOA Stewardship Program have phased out making and importing PFOA in the United States, while companies outside the program may still produce, import and use it.
    Source: U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, “Fact Sheet: 2010/2015 PFOA Stewardship Program” (regulator), read 2026-09-30.
    Their words
    “Domestic production and import of PFOA has been phased out in the United States by the companies participating in the 2010/2015 PFOA Stewardship Program. Quantities of PFOA may be produced, imported, and used by companies not participating in the PFOA Stewardship Program” ('Manufacture and import of long-chain PFAS' section)
  5. Fluoropolymer makers in industrialized countries have moved to replace PFOA and PFNA with chemically similar substances.
    Source: Environmental Science & Technology (Lohmann et al., 2020), “Are Fluoropolymers Really of Low Concern for Human and Environmental Health and Separate from Other PFAS?” (peer-reviewed study), read 2026-09-30.
    Their words
    “Fluoropolymer producers in industrialized countries have moved to substitute PFOA and PFNA in polymer production with structurally similar alternatives such as per- and polyfluoroalkylether carboxylic acids (PFECAs).” (Section 3, 'Substitute fluoropolymer processing aids raise similar concerns')
  6. The US FDA lists nonstick coatings on pots and pans among the PFAS uses it has authorized for food contact. It says the coating is bonded to the pan at very high temperatures, and that studies show negligible amounts of PFAS in it can migrate to food.
    Source: U.S. Food and Drug Administration, “Authorized Uses of PFAS in Food Contact Applications” (regulator), read 2026-09-30.
    Their words
    “PFAS molecules are polymerized* (i.e., joined together to form large molecules) and are then applied to the surface of the cookware at very high temperatures, which tightly binds the polymer coating to the cookware. The manufacturing process vaporizes off virtually all the smaller (i.e., migratable) PFAS molecules. The result is a highly polymerized coating bound to the surface of the cookware. Studies show negligible amounts of PFAS in this coating can migrate to food.” (Table: 'Non-stick applications on pots & pans')
  7. A 2015 medical review of published cases of lung injury from PTFE fumes found that every patient had breathed fumes from PTFE overheated to between 391 and 875°C.
    Source: Journal of Medical Case Reports (Hamaya et al., 2015), “Polytetrafluoroethylene fume-induced pulmonary edema: a case report and review of the literature” (peer-reviewed study), read 2026-09-30.
    Their words
    “All patients had evidence of exposure to fumes developed from overheated (391 to 875°C) PTFE.” (Discussion, literature review of 18 cases)
  8. The Ecology Center also found that most pans labeled PFOA-free were coated with PTFE without clearly saying so.
    Source: Ecology Center (Healthy Stuff Lab), a Michigan nonprofit, “What’s Cooking? PFAS and Other Chemical Hazards in Nonstick Cooking and Baking Pans” (nonprofit research lab), read 2026-09-30.
    Their words
    “In fact, most pans labeled PFOA-free were coated with PTFE without clearly indicating that.” (Under the heading 'PFOA-free doesn’t mean PFAS-free')
