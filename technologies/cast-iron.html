How we decide a line's surface: The cooking surfaces of the frying pans and skillets the brand sells, in the brand's own words. We say PTFE or ceramic only where the brand's own page does (Teflon, a trademark for PTFE coatings, counts as naming PTFE); otherwise the coating is 'not named'.

What it is and what is known

  1. Cast iron is iron with a lot of carbon in it: the U.S. National Park Service puts it at no less than 1.7% carbon, usually 3.0 to 3.7%.
    Source: U.S. National Park Service, “Preservation Brief 27: The Maintenance and Repair of Architectural Cast Iron” (government research), read 2026-09-30.
    Their words
    “Cast iron is an alloy with a high carbon content (at least 1.7% and usually 3.0 to 3.7%)” (What is Cast Iron?)
  2. Lodge describes seasoning as oil baked onto the pan, through a process called polymerization.
    Source: Lodge Cast Iron, “How to Season” (the brand's own words, not independent), read 2026-09-30.
    Their words
    “Seasoning is just oil baked onto the pan through a process called polymerization” (Seasoning 101)
  3. Lodge, which makes both, says its carbon steel pans are formed by spinning and stamping, while its cast iron is made in sand molds.
    Source: Lodge Cast Iron, “Cast Iron & Carbon Steel” (the brand's own words, not independent), read 2026-09-30.
    Their words
    “Unlike cast iron, which is made using sand molds, our carbon steel cookware is formed by spinning and stamping.”
  4. In a 1986 study of twenty foods, most (90%) contained significantly more iron when cooked in iron pans than in non-iron ones.
    Source: Journal of the American Dietetic Association, abstract via the U.S. National Library of Medicine (PubMed), “Iron content of food cooked in iron utensils (Brittin HC, Nossaman CE; J Am Diet Assoc 1986;86(7):897-901)” (peer-reviewed study), read 2026-09-30.
    Their words
    “Most of the foods (90%) contained significantly more iron when cooked in iron utensils than when cooked in non-iron utensils.”
  5. The same study found that a food's acidity, moisture and cooking time all affected how much iron it picked up from an iron pan.
    Source: Journal of the American Dietetic Association, abstract via the U.S. National Library of Medicine (PubMed), “Iron content of food cooked in iron utensils (Brittin HC, Nossaman CE; J Am Diet Assoc 1986;86(7):897-901)” (peer-reviewed study), read 2026-09-30.
    Their words
    “Acidity, moisture content, and cooking time of food significantly affected the iron content of food cooked in iron utensils.”
  6. A 2021 systematic review found more iron, and more of it usable by the body, in food cooked in iron pots or with iron ingots.
    Source: Nepal Journal of Epidemiology, via PubMed Central, “Effect of cooking food in iron-containing cookware on increase in blood hemoglobin level and iron content of the food: A systematic review (Sharma et al., Nepal J Epidemiol 2021)” (peer-reviewed study), read 2026-09-30.
    Their words
    “Significant improvement in amount of iron in food and iron bioavailability was also observed when food was cooked using iron pot or ingots.” (Abstract)
