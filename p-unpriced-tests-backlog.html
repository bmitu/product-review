Claims nobody has measured

These are claims where no evidence exists in either direction — not disputed, just unmeasured. Each one is a test we could run if enough readers want it. Where a price is shown, it is built from a laboratory's own published schedule and named in the record; where it says not yet priced, no lab publishes a price for that workflow and we will not guess one — unpriced means unquoted, not cheap.

Caraway Ceramic Cookware Set — Partly measured — never tested in use

Caraway says: “Thoughtfully crafted without PTFE, PFOA, and PFAS, our proprietary non-stick ceramic offers effortless cooking, easy cleanup, and peace of mind at every meal.”

their own page · read 2026-09-23

UNSUPPORTED — we went and checked; here is what we found · checkable only by a new independent measurement

Bottom line. Caraway publishes lab results for its ceramic coating: no PFAS detected in the samples it sent. Nobody has measured what a pan sheds after years of real cooking, and no lab sells that test off the shelf.

What would settle it: Food-simulant migration testing on pans put through an accelerated service life — 200 heat cycles to 230 °C, 50 dishwasher cycles and standardised abrasion — assaying total organic fluorine and the 20+ heavy metals in the simulant, against unused controls from the same lot.
Estimated cost: not yet priced

Evidence

  • measurement — mixed, funded by Caraway

    https://app.lightlabs.com/comprehensive-testing-report/154/938

    Fetched Caraway's Light Labs report on its ceramic coating: PFAS not detected in submitted samples, with no statement of the samples' age or use.

    This is real testing and it answers half of the claim. ‘Without PTFE, PFOA, and PFAS’ is a statement about what the coating is made of, and on the samples Caraway sent, the lab found none of the 660 PFAS it looked for. ‘Peace of mind at every meal’ is a statement about years of use, and the report says in its own words that it does not reach that far: it describes a snapshot of submitted samples and disclaims future stability. The closest thing to a use test in it is a one-time soak in food simulants, which is not a pan that has been cooked on.

“At every meal” is a claim about a pan's whole service life, and no test of an unused pan reaches it. That is the gap every ceramic-coating argument actually turns on, and it is the one nobody has funded — every cookware dataset we have found so far was paid for by the brand selling the pan, Caraway's own included. It is also the measurement where a brand-funded result would be least believable, which is precisely why it stays unmade.

It is an accelerated-ageing step and a migration step at two different laboratories with an untested handoff between them, so no lab quotes it as one job. The figures below are assembled from published component prices — a build-up, not a quote, and we will not print it as though it were one.

FX as of 2026-09-18: EUR/USD 1.1483, HKD/USD 0.127465. • Accelerated service life — SGS publishes EN 12983-1 at HK$4,700 and EN 12983-2 (ceramic cookware) at HK$3,700, 9–13 days with coating. HK$8,400 ≈ $1,071. This is a PROXY: EN 12983 is a standardised compliance battery, not the 200-heat-cycle / 50-dishwasher / abrasion protocol described above. Overlapping, not equivalent. • Migration — Measurlabs publishes ‘Migration of heavy metals from ceramics, glass, and enameled articles’ at €190/sample. 6 × €190 = €1,140 ≈ $1,309. One cheaper number that does NOT apply, recorded so nobody budgets against it: the University of Arizona ALEC core facility lists total organic fluorine at $82.00/sample, about a fifth of the commercial rate — but that is internal pricing requiring a University of Arizona account, and the facility says costs differ for outside users. GreenPan Thermolon ceramic nonstick coating — Nobody has measured this

GreenPan says: “Even if heated to 850⁰F (454⁰C), our Thermolon coatings do not emit any toxic fumes.”

their own page · read 2026-09-20

UNSUPPORTED — no qualifying evidence · checkable only by a new independent measurement

Bottom line. GreenPan says its coating releases nothing toxic even at 850°F. We found no published measurement of what actually comes off a ceramic pan at that heat — not a contradiction, an absence, and one a single test could start to close.

What would settle it: Heat production-run coated pans to 454 °C (850 °F) in a controlled chamber and capture what comes off the surface: volatile and semi-volatile organic compounds speciated by thermal desorption gas chromatography–mass spectrometry, with separate capture for hydrogen fluoride and for ultrafine particles, across three production lots — and publish the resulting compound list together with the detection limit for each analyte, so that what was looked for and not found is as visible as what was found.
Estimated cost: not yet priced

Evidence

  • None exists. Nobody has run the measurement described above — that is the finding.

This is the claim in the set we would most like to see measured, and the one nobody appears to have measured.

It is worth being clear about why the question exists at all. The reason anyone asks what a hot pan gives off is the older problem with traditional non-stick: heat a fluoropolymer coating far enough and it begins to break down and release fumes. Ceramic coatings were brought to market as the answer to precisely that, and the sentence above is the strong form of the answer. The awkward part is that the published emissions work we could find is on fluoropolymer-coated pans, not ceramic ones. The method exists and has been applied to coated cookware — thermal desorption gas chromatography–mass spectrometry, which heats a sample and identifies what comes off it, is documented in the peer-reviewed literature for exactly this purpose. It appears not to have been turned on this kind of coating and published.

Two features of the sentence make it harder to close than it looks. The first is the word "any", which is an absolute: a test that finds nothing has established nothing was found above its detection limit, which is not the same as nothing being there. That gap is unavoidable, and it is why the test described below insists the limits be published alongside the results — a "not detected" whose threshold is hidden can mean almost anything. The second is "toxic", which is not defined here and has no single technical meaning. A measurement can produce a list of compounds and their quantities; deciding which of them count as toxic, and at what exposure, is a further judgement that the compound list alone does not make.

So the honest position is not that the claim is doubtful. It is that it is currently unmeasured, that a reader has been offered an absolute with no number attached, and that the measurement which would put a number on it is one we know how to specify.

On price: this one is not priced, and that is a finding rather than an omission. No laboratory we found publishes a rate for this workflow. The published figures that exist nearby cover single analytical runs on prepared samples — one laboratory caps volatile-compound analysis on collection tubes at $468 a sample, and general gas chromatography–mass spectrometry work is commonly quoted between roughly $150 and $800 a sample — but none of those cover the part that makes this test what it is: heating whole pans to 454 °C in a chamber built to capture the output. Adding those numbers together would produce a figure that looks researched and is invented. Unpriced here means unquoted, and unquoted is not the same as cheap.

Evidence

  • document — mixed, funded by Hestan Culinary

    https://hestanculinary.com/pages/product-disclosures

    Hestan's California and Colorado disclosure page, read in full: both chemical tables and the section saying which product lines each covers.

    This is the document that makes the claim look like a contradiction, and it is worth being exact about why it is not one. California's cookware law asks a brand to disclose listed chemicals in the handle or the food-contact surface. Hestan files one table for every stainless line it sells, NanoBond included, and nickel is on it. The same product page tells us the pan is built on 18/10 stainless steel, the grade named for its roughly 10% nickel. So the disclosure tells a reader that nickel is in the handle or the cooking surface of something in that group: the stainless cookware lines, the ovenware, the roasters, the tools and the accessories. It does not say which part of which product. Nickel in a handle, or in any of those other lines, would put it on that table just as surely as nickel in the NanoBond cooking surface, and the claim is only about that surface.

  • our own desk check — mixed, we are not a neutral party here

    https://hestanculinary.com/products/nanobond-clad-skillet-fry-pan

    The NanoBond skillet page read in full, plus a search of Hestan's own site for any test of the cooking surface; none was found.

    Every time the claim appears, Hestan attaches it to the titanium surface, never to the whole pan. The badge that reads 'Made without PFAS & Nickel' sits directly under 'Non Reactive Titanium Finish', so even the shortest version is tied to the finish. We read it that way, because that is how the brand writes it.

    Read that way, the claim is about a layer of titanium thousands of nano-layers thick on top of a nickel-bearing steel. Whether nickel from the steel beneath reaches, or leaches through, that layer is a question about the surface itself. Nothing Hestan publishes, and nothing we could find anywhere else, answers it.

This one looked like a catch and turned out not to be. At first glance the pieces fit a contradiction neatly. A pan sold as 'made without nickel' is built on 18/10 stainless steel, and the brand's own California chemical disclosure for its stainless cookware lists nickel twice.

It does not hold up, and the reason is scope. Hestan never says the pan is made without nickel. It says the titanium cooking surface is, every time. The disclosure, meanwhile, is filed once for all of its stainless lines, ovenware, tools and accessories together, and covers handles as well as cooking surfaces. The nickel on that table can come from any of those, and nothing on it places nickel in the titanium surface of this pan.

That leaves a narrower question that reading cannot answer: does any nickel reach the food from a titanium surface bonded onto nickel-bearing steel, new or after years of metal utensils and dishwasher cycles? Hestan's own description, a surface 'four times harder' than stainless steel and safe for metal utensils, is a claim about exactly that durability. It is a fair question for anyone who has avoided stainless steel because of a nickel allergy, which is the reader this claim is really written for. It is also a cheap one to test, and nobody appears to have tested it.

OLIPOP Vintage Cola, 12 fl oz (355 mL) — Nobody has measured this

their own page · read 2026-09-20

UNSUPPORTED — no qualifying evidence · checkable only by a new independent measurement

Bottom line. The can advertises more fiber, but how much of that fiber is the prebiotic kind is a trade secret. One lab assay per batch — about $360 — would turn it into a number anyone can check.

What would settle it: Quantify total fructans (inulin + FOS) in sealed cans by AOAC 997.08 (HPLC-RI), three production lots, reported against the declared 9 g of total dietary fiber — turning the undisclosed prebiotic fraction of OLISMART into a published number.
Estimated cost: $1,080

Evidence

  • None exists. Nobody has run the measurement described above — that is the finding.

This is the cheapest decisive test we have found. One assay, one product, one number, and the claim on the next record stops being un-dividable and becomes arithmetic a reader can redo. ‘More fiber per can’ is what the assay bites on: a dose-benefit claim whose dose is published only in aggregate.

No brand will pay for it, and that is structural rather than cynical. The only thing the result can do is narrow a claim the brand currently gets to leave open. That is the signature of a test that only readers will ever fund.

THE PRICE, and where it comes from. $1,080 = 3 production lots × $360.00, which is Medallion Labs' published per-analysis price for Fructans/Inulin by AOAC 997.08 HPLC-RI (7–9 business days, 15 g sample). The figure appears identically in Medallion's 2026 price catalog and on its per-test page, so it is the lab's own standing price for exactly this analysis. It is not a quote against our samples — no request was sent — and the catalog notes that prices are subject to change without notice.

Two bracketing sources, for honesty about how firm that number is. LA Testing / EMSL lists fructan (FOS + inulin) by HPLC at $460–920 per sample and by spectrophotometric method at $300–600 — but that schedule is Rev 8.1 dated 2011-07-29, fifteen years stale, so treat it as a bracket and not a price. Measurlabs does not offer fructan or inulin analysis at all; its dietary fiber determination is AOAC 991.43 inside a €380 nutritional package. Two of the three sources converge on Medallion's figure being the current one, and the third cannot do the test.

A DESIGN OPTION we have priced but not included above. The 9 g on the label is TOTAL dietary fiber, and the method behind that number is not disclosed. Medallion states that its Rapid Integrated Method (AOAC 2022.01, $580/analysis) ‘can accurately analyze samples that contain RS2/RS3 fibers, Oligosaccharides, Inulin/Fructans, Polydextrose, and a variety of other fiber ingredients’ — which implies the choice of fiber method matters when fructans are present. Re-measuring the denominator alongside the numerator, 3 lots × ($360 + $580), would cost $2,820 and would check the label's own 9 g rather than taking it on trust. It roughly triples the target, so it is a separate decision.
