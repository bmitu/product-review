PFAS in cookware: what the brands claim, and what they can show

Every cookware brand in this set makes a PFAS claim on its own page. The claims are not the interesting part — who paid for the evidence behind them is. Here is each claim, its source, and the funder of every document backing it.

Caraway — Holds up — but only on the seller's own evidence

Caraway says: “We don't just claim to be PFAS-free. We prove it—and we publish the reports.”

their own page · read 2026-09-23

SUPPORTED — evidence exists; nobody independent has measured the product · checkable by reading a document

Bottom line. Caraway does publish its reports: the page it names embeds a lab report on its ceramic coating, with every PFAS result not detected. Caraway paid for it, and none of the PFAS results says how low the test could see.

Evidence

  • measurement — supports, funded by Caraway

    https://app.lightlabs.com/comprehensive-testing-report/154/938

    Fetched the Light Labs report embedded on Caraway's testing page: ceramic coating, all 660 PFAS and total organic fluorine reported not detected.

    The report is real, published, and on the page Caraway names. Two things it does not say. First, what ‘not detected’ means in numbers: the heavy-metal and extraction results each give a limit (lead below 0.002 mg/kg, for instance), but none of the 660 PFAS results or the total-fluorine result states the level below which the lab stops seeing. Second, what was tested: the lab describes its results as an analysis of ‘submitted samples’ and never says whether they were new, used, or from which production lot. Caraway paid for the testing, which is ordinary and which is why this cannot count as independent confirmation.

  • our own desk check — supports, we are not a neutral party here

    https://www.carawayhome.com/third-party-testing

    The address the claim names redirects to Caraway's testing page, which embeds the lab's report as an iframe from the lab's own domain.

    We got this wrong the first time, and the error is worth showing. Our 20 September check read the page Caraway named and found no reports in it. The reports were never in that page: it loads them from the lab's own site, so they appear in a browser and nowhere in the page's own code. Our search could not see them, and we said at the time that a browser might. We should have looked for exactly that before publishing a finding.

CORRECTION, 23 September 2026. An earlier version of this record said we found no reports on the page Caraway names. That was wrong. The page embeds them from the testing lab's own site, which our check could not see. The verdict has changed from unsubstantiated to supported.

What still matters, and did before the correction: ‘not detected’ is not the same sentence as ‘zero’. An assay that stops looking at 100 parts per billion and one that stops at 1 will both print ‘not detected’, and they are not the same result. This report gives that number for heavy metals and for the extraction tests. For the PFAS, which are what the claim is about, it does not.

Cuisinart Chemical Disclosures page — Does not hold up

Cuisinart says: “PFAS, used in cookware due to its high performance as a non-stick surface, is authorized by the FDA for use in paper/paperboard food packaging, nonstick cookware and other food contact applications.”

their own page · read 2026-09-27

SUPPORTED — evidence exists; nobody independent has measured the product · checkable by reading a document

Bottom line. Cuisinart says PFAS are FDA-authorized for paper food packaging, nonstick cookware and other uses. The FDA page it cites says the paper authorizations were withdrawn in January 2025. The nonstick part holds.

Evidence

  • document — refutes, independent

    https://www.fda.gov/food/process-contaminants-food/authorized-uses-pfas-food-contact-applications

    The FDA page Cuisinart cites, read in full (current as of 3 January 2025): PFAS authorizations for paper packaging are no longer effective.

    Cuisinart links this page as its source. The page supports half the sentence: PFAS are authorized for nonstick cookware and some other food-contact uses. It contradicts the other half. The paper and paperboard authorizations the sentence leads with have been withdrawn, and the page says so.

  • our own desk check — refutes, we are not a neutral party here

    https://www.cuisinart.com/chemical-disclosure

    Cuisinart's Chemical Disclosures page read in full: the sentence is live and links the FDA page above.

    The sentence may have been accurate when it was written: before 2024 the paper authorizations were in force. The page carries no date, and the sentence was live on 27 September 2026, well after the FDA withdrew them.

This sentence is mostly background, but it is wrong in a way a reader can check from Cuisinart's own link. The FDA page it cites says PFAS grease-proofers for paper and paperboard stopped being sold in the US in 2024, and that the FDA declared their authorizations no longer effective in January 2025. The rest of the sentence holds: the FDA does authorize certain PFAS in nonstick coatings.

It matters because of where it sits. The sentence introduces Cuisinart's chemical disclosures, and its job is to reassure. Leading with an authorization the regulator has withdrawn makes PFAS sound more settled than the FDA's own page does. It is not a claim about any Cuisinart pan, and it says nothing about whether any pan is safe.

It adds that in January 2025 the FDA announced 'that the 35 food contact notifications (FCNs) related to PFAS-containing food contact substances as grease-proofers applied to paper and paperboard food packaging are no longer effective based on the abandonment of these uses', and a note says those authorizations 'have been revoked by the FDA due to abandonment'. GreenPan — Holds up — but only on the seller's own evidence

GreenPan says: “Lastly, as part of our compliance with California’s AB1200 bill, any of the 3,000+ designated substances of concern that need to be disclosed are done so and listed openly on our website, GreenPan.us, meaning that, contrary to what some articles allege, GreenPan provides a high level of disclosure.”

their own page · read 2026-09-20

SUPPORTED — evidence exists; nobody independent has measured the product · checkable by reading a document

Bottom line. GreenPan says it lists its California-required chemical disclosures openly on its site, and it does — a live page names them, product line by product line. Whether that list is complete is not something reading it can settle.

Evidence

  • document — supports, funded by GreenPan (The Cookware Company (USA), LLC)

    https://www.greenpan.us/pages/california-ab1200-disclosure

    The full disclosure page with all ten of its tables, readable in the HTML the server sends without browser scripting.

    The disclosure is published by the brand itself, which is exactly what the law asks for and exactly why it cannot carry this record to the top tier. A company's own statement about its own products can be accurate and still be the company's own statement. What raises it above an unsupported assertion is that it is specific, itemised and checkable: it names substances rather than reassurances, and it cites the regulatory list behind each one, so a reader who disagrees has something concrete to disagree with.

  • our own desk check — supports, we are not a neutral party here

    https://www.greenpan.us/pages/california-ab1200-disclosure

    Our own search for the page: address guessing proved nothing and was discarded; the site's own footer link is what locates it.

    The claim names a place, so the check is to go to it. Two commands reproduce the whole finding:

    curl -sL 'https://www.greenpan.us/' | grep -c 'california-ab1200-disclosure'
1

curl -sL 'https://www.greenpan.us/pages/california-ab1200-disclosure' | grep -c '<table'
10

    The first says the disclosure is one link from the front page, in the footer that appears on every page of the site. That is the most findable place it could be — not search-only, not buried in a support article. The second says what is behind the link.

    What we found is not a gesture at transparency. It is ten separate tables, one per product line, each naming chemicals and the regulatory list each one appears on — aluminium, total chromium and chromium compounds, nickel and nickel compounds, iron, manganese compounds, copper, zinc, lead, tin, cobalt (II) oxide, cadmium compounds, antimony compounds and others, each tied to the list that flags it, several linking through to the state's own chemical records.

    The page also draws a line we would have drawn ourselves: it states that the food-contact surface of the ceramic nonstick contains none of these chemicals, and that what it is disclosing are chemicals that may be present in the layers beneath that surface and in the stainless-steel handles. That distinction is the useful part. A reader who wants to know what touches the food and a reader who wants to know what is in the object are asking different questions, and the page answers both separately instead of blurring them.

This is the first claim we have checked that survived the check, and it is worth saying plainly why that matters. The usual outcome when a brand points at its own receipts is that the receipts are not there. Here they were. A reader can reach them in one click from the footer of any page on the site, and what they find names chemicals rather than describing a commitment to safety.

What we verified is that the disclosure exists, is public, is itemised per product line, and separates the food-contact surface from the layers and handles beneath it. What we did not verify, and could not verify by reading, is the part of the sentence doing the heaviest lifting: that everything which needs disclosing has been disclosed. Completeness is a claim about what is absent from a list, and no amount of reading a list establishes that. Settling it would take composition testing of the products themselves, which is a different and far more expensive exercise than the one we did here.

So the fair reading is narrower than the sentence and still favourable to it: the brand said the disclosure is there, and the disclosure is there.

One thing that will date this. California added microplastics to the same candidate-chemical list on 18 June 2026, with the listing taking effect on 1 October 2026, and several law firms tracking the rule read that as pulling microplastics into the cookware disclosure duty this autumn. We were not able to confirm those dates against the department's own pages — every request we sent to its site was refused by its firewall — so they are reported here as the firms state them, not as we checked them. The consequence for the page above is straightforward: a disclosure that is complete in September may not be complete in October, and this record should be read again after that date.
