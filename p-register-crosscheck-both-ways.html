Two brands, the same check, opposite answers

Both of these claims were checked the same way, for nothing. A company points at a public list kept by a regulator. The regulator publishes that list. You download it and you count.

We ran that on two cookware brands without knowing in advance what either would show. One brand's number was right when it was written and has been quietly wrong since. The other brand's disclosure came back right twelve times out of twelve on one official list, twelve out of twelve on a second, and eleven out of twelve on each of four more — sixty-eight of seventy-two in all. All four misses land on the same line of the same table, and none is a gap we can pin on anybody, because the one database that would settle it is the one California refuses to serve.

We are publishing them together on purpose. A brand does not get a good verdict here for being likeable and does not get a bad one for being a target; it gets the verdict the register gives. The method was fixed before either answer was known, which is the only reason the pair means anything.

The part worth taking away is not about either company. Every check below cost one download and an afternoon. Nobody had run them.

GreenPan Thermolon ceramic nonstick coating — Does not hold up

GreenPan says: “We confirm that GreenPan’s™ Thermolon™ coated food contact surface is Free Of: … • And 247 Substances of Very High Concern (SVHC) that are listed under the European REACH (Registration, Evaluation, Authorization and Restriction of Chemicals) Program including the siloxanes of concern, namely D4, D5, D6 and Octamethyltrisiloxane”

their own page · read 2026-09-20

SUPPORTED — evidence exists; nobody independent has measured the product · checkable by reading a document

Bottom line. GreenPan says its coating is free of the 247 Substances of Very High Concern on Europe’s REACH list. That list held 247 entries for five months in 2025 and holds 253 now. The figure was right when written; the page never says when that was.

Evidence

  • document — refutes, independent

    https://chem.echa.europa.eu/api-obligation-list/v1/candidateList?pageIndex=1&pageSize=200

    The chemicals agency's full Candidate List, all 253 dated entries, matching the register's own stated total with every row carrying a distinct identifier.

    The claim names a number that can simply be counted, and the body that maintains the list publishes it. Anyone can repeat this in two commands:

    curl -s 'https://chem.echa.europa.eu/api-obligation-list/v1/candidateList?pageIndex=1&pageSize=200' -o p1.json
curl -s 'https://chem.echa.europa.eu/api-obligation-list/v1/candidateList?pageIndex=2&pageSize=200' -o p2.json

    Each file reports the register’s own total alongside its rows, which is what makes the count checkable rather than merely plausible: if the rows you receive do not add up to the total the register states, you know your download is short before you publish a number from it.

    The result, and the reason the date matters. The list today holds 253 entries. It held 247 between 21 January 2025 and 25 June 2025, and 247 at no other time — before that date there were 242, and after it 250. So the figure on the cookware page is not wrong in the way a typo is wrong. It is an accurate reading of the register taken during a five-month window that closed fifteen months ago, printed in the present tense with no date attached.

    Six entries have been added since:

    25 Jun 2025  1,1,1,3,5,5,5-heptamethyl-3-[(trimethylsilyl)oxy]trisiloxane
25 Jun 2025  Decamethyltetrasiloxane
25 Jun 2025  a reactive textile dye (Reactive Brown 51)
05 Nov 2025  1,1'-(ethane-1,2-diyl)bis[pentabromobenzene]
04 Feb 2026  n-hexane
04 Feb 2026  bisphenol AF and its salts

    Two of those six are siloxanes, added for the same reason — very persistent and very bioaccumulative — as the siloxane the page already names. That is the detail worth a reader’s attention, because the sentence does not stop at a number: it goes on to name “the siloxanes of concern” and lists four of them. The register agrees about all four. D4, D5 and D6 were listed in June 2018, and octamethyltrisiloxane in January 2025. What the page does not say is that the family it singled out grew by two more members five months later.

    One further thing the dates give us, which is unusual in this kind of check. Octamethyltrisiloxane joined the list on 21 January 2025 — the very day the total reached 247. The number and the substance became true together, so the sentence cannot have been written before that date and has not been revisited since the following June. The claim carries no date, but its own contents date it.

  • our own desk check — mixed, we are not a neutral party here

    https://www.greenpan.us/pages/safety-of-our-product

    Our own fetch of the page on the day quoted; it returned successfully and carries no publication or revision date anywhere.

    Worth being exact about what our own reading of the page does and does not settle. It settles the wording, and it settles that no date is offered with it. It does not settle the other reading available to a fair critic — that “247” might describe a testing panel of 247 named substances rather than the whole list. We think that reading is hard to sustain, because 247 is exactly the register’s total for a specific five-month window and the substance the sentence names was added on the day that window opened. But it is not impossible, and if it were the intended meaning the claim would not be out of date so much as unverifiable, since the 247 substances are never listed.

This is the cheapest kind of check there is, and it is worth showing exactly because it costs nothing. A brand prints a number. The body that maintains the number publishes it. You count.

GreenPan’s safety statement says its coated cooking surface is free of “247 Substances of Very High Concern (SVHC) that are listed under the European REACH” programme. The European Chemicals Agency keeps that list, adds to it a few times a year, and stamps every entry with the date it was included. We downloaded all 253 current entries and counted them, then used the inclusion dates to count what the list held on any past date.

247 is real. It is the size of the list between 21 January 2025 and 25 June 2025 — and at no other point in the list’s history. Today the figure is 253. So the sentence was accurate when someone wrote it, and it has been left standing, in the present tense, through three subsequent updates to the thing it describes.

We want to be careful about what that does and does not mean, because it would be easy to read this as a bigger accusation than it is. Nothing here suggests the coating contains any of the six substances added since. We have not tested the coating and neither, so far as anyone outside the company can tell, has any laboratory whose report you can read — which is the subject of a separate finding. A claim to be free of 247 substances and a claim to be free of 253 are, chemically, almost the same claim.

What it does mean is narrower and, we think, more useful. A “free of” list is a promise of completeness against an outside standard, and the whole value of pinning it to a regulator’s list is that the list is maintained. A number copied out of a maintained list and then frozen keeps the authority of the source while quietly losing the currency — which is the part the reader was relying on. Six entries have been added since, and two of them are siloxanes listed as very persistent and very bioaccumulative: the same family, and the same stated reason, as the siloxane the sentence itself names. The claim is most out of date precisely where it chose to be most specific.

In fairness to GreenPan, and this cuts the other way: everything the sentence names, the register confirms. D4, D5, D6 and octamethyltrisiloxane are all on the list, the first three since 2018 and the fourth since January 2025. This is not a brand inventing regulatory cover. It is a brand that got it right and then stopped looking — and, because the page carries no date, gave its readers no way to notice.

The fix is a sentence long: print the date the count was taken, or drop the number and name the list. “Every substance on the REACH Candidate List” cannot go stale.

Every row carries a distinct registry identifier, so nothing was counted twice. T-fal PTFE nonstick cookware — Does not hold up

T-fal says: “The World Health Organization (WHO), the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), and health authorities in France, Europe (EFSA), and the United States (FDA) have all confirmed that PTFE is harmless to human health.”

their own page · read 2026-09-26

SUPPORTED — evidence exists; nobody independent has measured the product · checkable by reading a document

Bottom line. T-fal says IARC confirmed PTFE is harmless. IARC put PTFE in Group 3, which by IARC's own definition means it could not classify its cancer risk and is not a finding of safety. We checked the IARC part only.

Evidence

  • document — refutes, independent

    https://monographs.iarc.who.int/list-of-classifications

    IARC's list of classifications, read from the data the page loads: polytetrafluoroethylene is in Group 3, evaluated in 1987.

    IARC evaluates one question only: whether something can cause cancer. It looked at PTFE in a 1979 volume of its Monographs and again in its 1987 update, and placed it in Group 3. That is the group for agents it cannot classify either way. IARC has not said anything about PTFE being harmless, because that is not a question it answers.

  • document — refutes, independent

    https://monographs.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Preamble-2019.pdf

    IARC's Preamble, the document that defines its groups, downloaded and read for the Group 3 definition.

    IARC's own definition of Group 3 is 'not classifiable as to its carcinogenicity to humans', and the Preamble goes on: 'An evaluation in Group 3 is not a determination of non-carcinogenicity or overall safety. It often means that the agent is of unknown carcinogenic potential and that there are significant gaps in research.' That is close to the opposite of the confirmation T-fal describes.

  • our own desk check — refutes, we are not a neutral party here

    https://www.t-fal.com/ptfe-nonstick-coating-explained

    T-fal's PTFE explainer page read in full on the same day; it describes the IARC classification twice, both times as an all-clear.

    T-fal cites the right volumes and the right group, then reads the group backwards. Group 3 does not recognise that PTFE is not carcinogenic. It says IARC could not classify it.

This is not a claim about whether PTFE nonstick pans are safe to cook with. It is a claim about what a named authority said, and that can be checked by reading what the authority said.

IARC's job is narrow. It asks whether something can cause cancer and sorts the answer into groups. It has never assessed PTFE's overall safety, because it assesses nothing but cancer. On cancer, it placed PTFE in the group reserved for agents it cannot classify, and it says in plain words that this group is not a finding of safety. T-fal's page cites the right volume and the right group, then presents the group as a clean bill of health.

The same sentence names the WHO, which is IARC's parent body, and food-safety regulators in France, Europe and the United States. We have not checked those. Food regulators do permit PTFE coatings in contact with food, which is a real and relevant fact, but permission to use a material is a regulatory decision, not a finding that it is harmless in every sense. Whether any of them used the word T-fal attributes to them is a separate question, and we have left it open rather than answer it by assumption.

How we checked

  • document — monographs.iarc.who.int

    Read on 2026-09-26. The list page builds its table in the browser from a data file IARC serves alongside it, and we read that file directly. Its entry reads: polytetrafluoroethylene, CAS 9002-84-0, Group 3, Monographs volume 19 and Supplement 7, evaluated 1987.

  • document — monographs.iarc.who.int

    Downloaded on 2026-09-26 and read for its definition of the evaluation groups. This is the current Preamble, adopted in 2019. PTFE was evaluated under an earlier one, but the Group 3 label, 'not classifiable as to its carcinogenicity to humans', is the same in both.

  • our own desk check — t-fal.com

    Read on 2026-09-26 in the HTML the server sends. Besides the sentence quoted above, the same page says: 'Following a study by the IARC, the WHO classified PTFE in Group 3 [Volume 19, 288 (1979) and Supplement 7.70 (1987)], recognizing that it is not carcinogenic to humans.'

Blue Lizard Sensitive Mineral Sunscreen Lotion SPF 50 (3 oz and 5 oz) — Holds up — but only on the seller's own evidence

Blue Lizard (Crown Laboratories) says: “We ♥ the Reef: This product is made without Oxybenzone or Octinoxate to help protect fragile reef ecosystems.”

their own page · read 2026-09-23

SUPPORTED — evidence exists; nobody independent has measured the product · checkable by reading a document

Bottom line. Blue Lizard says this sunscreen is made without oxybenzone or octinoxate, and the full ingredient list it filed with the government names neither. That is the maker's own filing, not a lab test.

Evidence

  • document — supports, funded by Crown Laboratories (the maker files its own label; the National Library of Medicine publishes it)

    https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=32d78048-d281-1d80-e063-6394a90aec19

    The full ingredient list the maker filed for this sunscreen names neither chemical; the same search finds oxybenzone on a label that contains it.

    Sunscreen in the US is regulated as a drug, so its Drug Facts label is a legal filing, not marketing copy, and the maker has to list every ingredient. That makes the filed label the right document for a 'made without' claim. Two commands reproduce the finding:

    curl -sL 'https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/services/v2/spls/32d78048-d281-1d80-e063-6394a90aec19.xml' | grep -ciE 'oxybenzone|benzophenone|octinoxate|methoxycinnamate'
0

curl -sL 'https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/services/v2/spls/598cfe9e-6ebe-ad18-e063-6294a90ace22.xml' | grep -ciE 'oxybenzone|benzophenone|octinoxate|methoxycinnamate'
4

    The first is this sunscreen, and the answer is zero. The second is an unrelated sunscreen that does contain oxybenzone, and the same search finds it, which shows the search works.

    What this is and is not matters. The label is the maker's own statement, filed with the government, and a false one would make the product misbranded under federal law. That is a strong reason for it to be accurate, but it is still the maker describing its own product. Nobody measured what is in the tube, including us. The label shows what the maker says it uses, not a lab result.

  • our own desk check — mixed, we are not a neutral party here

    https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=9d20b230-2cce-4d90-e053-2995a90a4c03

    The product page's barcodes belong to an older formula filed under a second label; that formula also names neither chemical.

    The product page describes one formula and scans as another. Its ingredient list, with aloe, matches the newer label. Its barcodes match the older one. Both labels are live on the government database, and the store does not say which formula a given order will contain.

    For this claim it does not matter: neither formula lists oxybenzone or octinoxate, so the claim holds whichever tube arrives. It would matter to anyone buying for a different reason, such as avoiding a particular preservative, because the ingredient list on the product page may not be the one on the tube. The label on the tube you receive is the one to read.

The claim has two halves, and we checked one. The first half is a fact about the formula: the product is made without two particular sunscreen chemicals. That can be checked, because US sunscreens are sold as drugs and the maker must publicly file a complete ingredient list. The filed list for this product names neither chemical, under any of their names, in either of the two formulas currently on file. The brand's wider statement that all Blue Lizard sunscreens are free of both also held: none of the 21 Blue Lizard labels on file names either one.

The second half, 'to help protect fragile reef ecosystems', is a statement about why, and about what leaving the two chemicals out does for coral. Hawaii banned both chemicals from sale for that reason, but whether a sunscreen without them is actually better for reefs is a question for marine science, not for a label. This record does not decide it.

One practical note: the product page shows the newer formula's ingredients but carries the older formula's barcodes, and both formulas are still on file. If you are choosing this sunscreen for reasons beyond these two chemicals, read the label on the tube you receive.

How we checked

  • document — dailymed.nlm.nih.gov

    Read on 2026-09-23 from DailyMed, the National Library of Medicine's public database of drug labels, through its public API. Sunscreens are sold in the US as drugs, and the maker must file the label, with every active and inactive ingredient, in a structured format. This label is 'Blue Lizard Sensitive SPF 50 Sunscreen', effective 31 December 2025. Its active ingredients are zinc oxide (112 mg/mL) and titanium dioxide (89.6 mg/mL); it lists 28 inactive ingredients, including aloe leaf, matching the 'new and improved formula with soothing Aloe Vera' and the ingredient list on the brand's product page. As a control, the same search run on another maker's label that lists oxybenzone found it 4 times. We did not test the product.

  • our own desk check — dailymed.nlm.nih.gov

Those two package codes are not on the label cited above, which covers 0316-2046-31 (89 mL) and -46 (148 mL). They are on a second, live label for the same product name, effective 12 January 2026, covering -30 (89 mL) and -45 (148 mL). That label has the same active ingredients at the same strengths but a different set of 28 inactive ingredients: no aloe, and it includes phenoxyethanol and chlorphenesin. Caraway Cookware, bakeware, kettle, prep set, glass lids, kitchen gadgets and cast iron (California disclosure) — Holds up

Caraway says: “At Caraway, product safety is our utmost concern and we are fully committed to adhering to all relevant regulations governing our products. … The tables linked at the bottom of this webpage outline the cookware items that necessitate such disclosures. … The following items are listed to maintain compliance with California Bill AB-1200 and are compiled from the CalSafer Candidate Chemical list.”

their own page · read 2026-09-22

SUPPORTED — evidence exists; nobody independent has measured the product · checkable by reading a document

Bottom line. Caraway publishes the chemical disclosure California requires. Across six authoritative lists read in full it is right 68 times out of 72; all four misses sit on its cadmium row, and we cannot say whether the state or the company made them.

Evidence

  • document — supports, independent

    https://oehha.ca.gov/media/downloads/proposition-65/p65chemicalslist.pdf

    California's full Proposition 65 list, 23 pages dated 31 July 2026, checked A to Z against its own final page so nothing was cut short.

    The state publishes this list as a single file, and checking a company's homework against it costs nothing but the download:

    curl -sL 'https://oehha.ca.gov/media/downloads/proposition-65/p65chemicalslist.pdf' -o p65.pdf

    What we compared. Caraway's disclosure names twelve distinct substances across its product lines, gives a registry number for each, and states for each one which official lists it appears on. Three of the twelve are marked as appearing on Proposition 65: nickel, titanium oxide and cadmium. The other nine are not.

    All twelve agree with the state's list.

    Nickel appears as “Nickel (Metallic)”, with the same registry number Caraway gives, listed since October 1989. Cadmium and titanium dioxide are both on the list too, but neither carries a registry number there — cadmium appears twice, as “Cadmium” and as “Cadmium and cadmium compounds”, and titanium dioxide appears in a qualified form, “Titanium dioxide (airborne, unbound particles of respirable size)”. Searching by number alone returns nothing for either, which is worth saying plainly because it is the mistake this check was one step away from making: a company would have looked wrong on two of its three entries purely because of how the state records a family of substances.

    The nine unmarked substances are the more informative half, because getting those right requires care. Caraway lists chromium, and Proposition 65 lists chromium — but only hexavalent chromium, a different chemical form from the metal in stainless steel, and Caraway does not claim it. The same distinction holds three more times: the list carries vanadium pentoxide but not vanadium, molybdenum trioxide but not molybdenum, iron dextran complex but not iron. Manganese, phosphorus, ethyl acetate, boron and butyl alcohol are not on the state's list in any form. In every one of those nine cases the absence of a Proposition 65 mark is correct.

    So on the one authoritative list we could check end to end, the disclosure is accurate twelve times out of twelve, including four places where a careless reading of the state's list would have produced a wrong answer in the company's own favour or against it.

  • document — supports, independent

    https://monographs.iarc.who.int/list-of-classifications

    All 1,128 entries of the cancer agency's classification list; our copy matches its own published group totals of 135, 98, 329 and 498.

    California's rule does not treat the whole cancer agency list as a carcinogen list, and the difference decides this check. The regulation names "Groups 1, 2A, and 2B carcinogens identified by the International Agency for Research on Cancer". Group 3 — "not classifiable" — is deliberately left out, because being unclassifiable is not a finding of anything.

    All twelve entries agree with the list read that way.

    The three Caraway attributes to the cancer agency are right. Nickel is there twice, as nickel compounds in Group 1 and as metallic nickel in Group 2B, the second with exactly the registry number Caraway prints. Titanium dioxide is in Group 2B, again with the matching number. Cadmium and cadmium compounds are in Group 1, matching number.

    The nine Caraway does not attribute are right too, and one of them is the sharpest case we have seen in this kind of check. Chromium is on the cancer agency's list, with the exact registry number Caraway gives for it — as "chromium, metallic", in Group 3. A checker matching registry numbers would call this a missing attribution and accuse the company of leaving something out. The regulation says the opposite: Group 3 is not one of the groups it counts, so the correct action is not to claim it, which is what Caraway did.

    Five more are near-relatives that the list carries and the company did not claim: iron-dextran complex rather than iron, vanadium pentoxide rather than vanadium, molybdenum trioxide rather than molybdenum, phosphorus-32 rather than phosphorus, and tert-butyl alcohol rather than the different form of butyl alcohol Caraway names. Manganese, boron and ethyl acetate do not appear on the list in any form.

    So on a second authoritative list, checked end to end and independently of the first, the disclosure is again accurate twelve times out of twelve.

  • document — mixed, independent

    https://web.archive.org/web/20151005075534/http://ntp.niehs.nih.gov/ntp/roc/content/listed_substances_508.pdf

    The 2014 federal cancer report the rule names, read in full from an archived capture of the government's own address, alongside the current edition.

    This is the list where a gap shows up, and the first thing to say is that the odd-looking part of it is not the company's doing.

    Caraway's tables cite the "NTP 13th RoC" — the thirteenth edition of a federal cancer report, published in 2014 and superseded twice since, in 2016 and again in 2021. That looks like a company quoting something a decade out of date. It isn't. California's regulation itself names the thirteenth edition, by number, as the list it counts. The company is quoting the rule exactly. The rule is what stopped in 2014.

    We read that edition anyway, since it is the one that governs, and the current one alongside it.

    Nickel, the one substance Caraway attributes to this report, is on it — as nickel compounds, known to be human carcinogens, and as metallic nickel, reasonably anticipated to be one. That is right, and it is right in the current edition too.

    Cadmium is the gap. Cadmium and cadmium compounds are listed in the thirteenth edition as known to be human carcinogens — the strongest category the report has — and the company's own write-up for cadmium records it as first listed in 1980 and listed ever since, so no question of editions explains it. Caraway's cadmium row cites twelve separate lists, including the cancer agency's and California's own, and does not cite this one.

    We cannot tell you whose omission it is, and we are not going to guess. The disclosure says it was compiled from the state's candidate chemical database, which is the database that refused every attempt we made to read it. Either the company dropped a line, or the state's own record for cadmium is missing it and the company copied what was there. The one document that would settle it is the one California will not let the public open.

    The other ten substances are correctly absent, including the two the report carries in a different form: hexavalent chromium rather than the chromium in stainless steel, and iron dextran complex rather than iron.

  • document — mixed, independent

    http://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/02008R1272-20260701

    All 4,420 substances in the European classification table in force from 1 July 2026, fetched twice byte-for-byte identical with entry numbers unbroken.

    California's rule counts a narrow slice of the European list, and the slice decides this check. It counts substances the European Union classifies as causing cancer, inheritable genetic damage or reproductive harm in its two strongest categories, 1A and 1B, in the table annexed to its classification law. The category below those, 2, is not counted.

    Caraway attributes one substance to this list, and it is right — right down to the category letter it prints. The entry is diboron trioxide, and the classification the European Union has given it is reproductive toxicant, category 1B, which is exactly what the company's table says.

    It is worth saying what that correctness survived. Caraway names the substance “Boron” and gives the number 1303-86-2. Boron the element is not on this list at all, and that number is not boron's — it belongs to one of its oxides. The name is loose and the number is exact, so a reader checking the name would find nothing and conclude the company had invented the attribution. The number is what makes it true.

    Two of the substances Caraway does not attribute are on the European list, with the exact numbers the company prints, and both would read as missing attributions to anyone matching numbers. Nickel is there as a category 2 carcinogen. Titanium dioxide is there as a category 2 carcinogen too. Category 2 is not one of the categories California's rule counts, so in both cases the correct action is not to claim the list — which is what Caraway did.

    Cadmium is the gap, and it is the same row that came up short on the federal cancer report. Cadmium is on the European list as a category 1B carcinogen, in both the forms the list carries it in, and this is not a recent addition: a version of the law from December 2013, two months after California's rule took effect, classifies it identically, and prints beside it the older European classification it was translated from. Caraway's cadmium row does not cite this list.

    So that one row now misses two attributions, from two unrelated lists, while every other substance in the disclosure is right on all four. We still cannot tell you whose omission it is and we are still not going to guess. But the shape of it has changed. Eleven substances right four times over, and one substance wrong twice, points more at the record that row was copied from than at scattered carelessness. The disclosure says it was compiled from the state's candidate chemical database — the database that refused every attempt we made to read it. That is still the one document that would settle it, and it is still shut.

    The remaining eight are correctly absent. Three of them — ethyl acetate, butanol and phosphorus — are on the European list, but for catching fire and for irritation, not for cancer or reproductive harm. The other five are not on it in the form Caraway names. Chromium is the one to be careful with: chromium compounds in one particular chemical state are category 1A and 1B here, but the chromium in stainless steel is a different substance with a different number, and the list does not carry it.

    So on a fourth authoritative list, read end to end and independent of the other three, the disclosure is right eleven times out of twelve — forty-six of forty-eight across the four.

  • document — mixed, independent

    https://iris.epa.gov/AdvancedSearch/

    The EPA database's whole table of 746 safe-dose values for 528 substances, each with the effect it rests on, plus its 2013 cadmium file.

    California's rule does not count everything in this database. It counts "chemicals for which a reference dose or reference concentration has been developed based on neurotoxicity" — a safe daily dose, swallowed or breathed, that the agency set because of harm to the nervous system. The database says, for every figure it publishes, which effect the figure rests on. So the check is not whether a substance is in the database but why its number is there.

    Caraway attributes three substances to this list. Two are right.

    Manganese is right twice over: the swallowed figure rests on effects on the central nervous system and the breathed one on impaired neurobehavioural function, and the agency files both under the nervous system. Butyl alcohol is right: its figure rests on reduced activity and loss of coordination in test animals, also filed under the nervous system.

    Cadmium is not. The database holds one safe-dose figure for cadmium, for swallowing, and it rests on significant proteinuria — protein leaking into the urine, a sign of kidney damage. The agency files it under the urinary system. There is no breathed figure at all; the agency has never set one. Nothing in the cadmium file rests on the nervous system, and the 2013 copy we read says the same, so this is not a case of the list changing after the company wrote its table.

    That makes this the first error in the other direction. Every gap found so far was a list left off; this is a list claimed that the rule does not support.     Two things our own visit settles, and one it does not.

    It settles that the disclosure is findable. The disclosure says it was compiled from the state's candidate chemical list, which is held in a public database called CalSafer. We tried to read that database and could not. This is the check that costs nothing, run a second time on a second brand — and this time the company mostly passes.

California's AB 1200 requires cookware sold in the state to disclose, on the manufacturer's website, any listed chemical intentionally put into a handle or a food-contact surface. Caraway keeps a page for this and links a document from it. The document names twelve substances across eleven product lines, gives each a chemical registry number, says which part of which product it is in, and — the part that makes this checkable — states for each substance which official lists it appears on. Thirteen different lists are named across the tables.

Six of those thirteen publish openly enough to be read end to end for nothing. We have read all six.

On California's Proposition 65 list, all twelve entries are right. On the World Health Organization cancer agency's list, all twelve are right again. On the federal government's report on carcinogens, on the European Union's classification table, and on each of two federal lists of substances that harm the nervous system, eleven of the twelve are right. Seventy-two comparisons, sixty-eight right — and all four misses are on the same line of the same table.

The two clean passes took more care to confirm than they sound. Getting a list membership right is easy; getting the near-misses right is not, and this disclosure is full of them. Proposition 65 carries hexavalent chromium, not the chromium in stainless steel. It carries vanadium pentoxide, not vanadium; molybdenum trioxide, not molybdenum; iron dextran complex, not iron. The cancer agency's list repeats every one of those traps and adds phosphorus-32 for phosphorus and a different form of butyl alcohol. In every case the company claimed the substance it actually uses and not its close relative. Those are exactly the distinctions a disclosure written to look thorough rather than to be accurate would get wrong.

The sharpest case runs the other way. Chromium is on the cancer agency's list under the exact registry number Caraway prints — in Group 3, "not classifiable as to its carcinogenicity". California's rule counts Groups 1, 2A and 2B and deliberately leaves Group 3 out, so the right thing to do is not to claim it, which is what the company did. Anyone checking this by matching registry numbers would have accused the company of an omission that the regulation says is correct.

The gaps are all cadmium. The federal report lists cadmium and its compounds in its strongest category, known to be human carcinogens, and has done since 1980; the European table classifies it in the strongest category the rule counts. Caraway's cadmium row cites twelve separate lists and neither of those. On the two nervous-system lists the row carries one label, and it is on the wrong one: cadmium does not qualify for the list the row names, and on the date the company gives it did qualify for the one the row leaves out. Eleven substances are right on all six lists. Cadmium is right on two.

We are not going to tell you whose error that is, because we cannot find out. The disclosure says it was compiled from the state's candidate chemical database — and that database is the one thing in this whole check we could not open. Every attempt to read it was refused at the door. Either the company's line for cadmium is wrong, or the state's own record for cadmium is wrong and the company copied what was there. Four misses on one line and none on the other eleven looks much more like one bad record than like four separate slips — but that is a reading of the pattern, not something we can see. The document that would settle it is the document California will not let the public read.

There is one more thing worth saying, because it looks like a mistake and isn't. Caraway cites the thirteenth edition of the federal report, published in 2014 and replaced twice since. That is the company quoting California's regulation exactly: the regulation names the thirteenth edition, by number, as the list it counts. The rule is what stopped in 2014, not the company.

None of this is a finding about safety, and nothing here is a measurement of anything. Nickel, cadmium and titanium dioxide are disclosed as present in something — a stainless steel handle, a cast-iron coating. Disclosure is not a finding of harm and this record makes none. What was checked is whether the company described its own products accurately against public standards, and on sixty-eight of the seventy-two comparisons available to us, it did.

The contrast with the other cookware disclosure we have checked is the useful part. One company printed a regulatory number and left it standing until it went out of date. This one printed twelve entries against thirteen lists and got the fiddly ones right everywhere except one line. Both were checked the same way, for the same amount of money, in an afternoon. That is the point worth taking away: this kind of claim is checkable by anyone, and almost nobody checks it.

Certification claims are the ones we most often cannot check, for two reasons: the brand names a scheme without pointing to its own entry, or the scheme's public register will not open for anyone checking carefully. What that settles is narrow, and a reader should keep it narrow. The claim is that the product holds a particular certification, and it does.
